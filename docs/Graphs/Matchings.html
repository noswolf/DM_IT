Bipartite graphs

A simple graph G = (V, E) is bipartite if its vertex set can be split into two disjoint sets V₁ and V₂ so that every edge joins a vertex of V₁ to a vertex of V₂. The pair (V₁, V₂) is called a bipartition of V.

Bipartite graphs model pairing problems between two kinds of objects: students and projects, jobs and applicants, courses and teaching assistants. An edge means "this pair is acceptable". The question is always the same: how many pairs can we form at the same time, if nobody may be used twice?

Matchings

A matching M in a graph G is a subset of E in which no two edges share an endpoint. Each vertex is used by at most one edge of M.

A vertex that is an endpoint of an edge of M is matched in M. Otherwise it is unmatched (also called free). In these notes, matching edges are thick and gold, and matched vertices are filled in.

The empty set is always a matching, and so is any single edge.

Maximal versus maximum

These two words sound alike but mean different things.

  • A matching is maximal if no edge of G can be added to it. Every remaining edge touches a vertex that is already matched.
  • A matching is maximum if no matching of G has more edges. Its size is the matching number ν(G).

Every maximum matching is maximal, but a maximal matching can be much smaller than a maximum one. Choosing edges greedily, one at a time, only guarantees a maximal matching.

Complete and perfect matchings

Let G be bipartite with bipartition (V₁, V₂). A matching M is a complete matching from V₁ to V₂ if every vertex of V₁ is matched. Equivalently, |M| = |V₁|.

Since the |V₁| edges need different endpoints in V₂, a complete matching from V₁ to V₂ can only exist when |V₁| ≤ |V₂|.

A perfect matching matches every vertex of G. For a bipartite graph this means: complete from V₁ to V₂, and |V₁| = |V₂|.

Hall's Marriage Theorem

For A ⊆ V₁, the neighbourhood N(A) is the set of vertices of V₂ adjacent to at least one vertex of A.

Theorem (Hall, 1935). Let G be a bipartite graph with bipartition (V₁, V₂). Then G has a complete matching from V₁ to V₂ if and only if

|N(A)| ≥ |A| for every subset A ⊆ V₁.

The name comes from the original story. V₁ is a group of people, V₂ a group of possible partners, and an edge means the two are willing to marry. Everyone in V₁ can marry an acceptable partner exactly when every group of k people from V₁ has, together, at least k acceptable partners.

Why the condition is necessary (⇒). If M is complete, the vertices of A are matched to |A| different vertices, and all of them lie in N(A). So |N(A)| ≥ |A|.

Why it is sufficient (⇐), proof idea. Take a maximum matching M and suppose some u ∈ V₁ is unmatched. Let A be the vertices of V₁ that can be reached from u by alternating paths. Because M has no augmenting path (see below), every vertex of N(A) is matched to a vertex of A other than u. Hence |N(A)| = |A| − 1, which breaks Hall's condition. Part 3 shows you this set whenever the algorithm ends without a complete matching.

Augmenting paths

Let M be a matching. An alternating path is a path whose edges are alternately not in M and in M. An augmenting path is an alternating path that starts and ends at unmatched vertices.

An augmenting path P with 2k + 1 edges has k + 1 edges outside M and k edges in M. Swapping them gives the symmetric difference

M ⊕ P = (M − P) ∪ (P − M),

which is again a matching, with one more edge.

Theorem (Berge, 1957). A matching M is maximum if and only if there is no M-augmenting path.

So the algorithm "find an augmenting path, swap, repeat" always ends with a maximum matching. Part 3 runs it step by step.

      Exercise 6. Why every subset matters

      Construct a bipartite graph with |V₁| = |V₂| = 3 in which |N(A)| ≥ |A| holds for every A ⊆ V₁ with |A| ≤ 2, but there is still no complete matching from V₁ to V₂. Build it in Part 2 with Edit edges and confirm with Part 4.

      Show solution

      Let V₁ = {a, b, c}, V₂ = {x, y, z}, and join each of a, b, c to both x and y. Leave z isolated.

      Every set of one or two vertices of V₁ has neighbourhood {x, y} of size 2, so the condition holds for |A| ≤ 2. But N({a, b, c}) = {x, y} has size 2 < 3. So no complete matching exists.

      Exercise 7. The easy direction of Hall's theorem

      Prove: if a bipartite graph has a complete matching from V₁ to V₂, then |N(A)| ≥ |A| for every A ⊆ V₁.

      Show solution

      Let M be a complete matching. For each a ∈ A, let m(a) be the vertex of V₂ matched to a. Since a and m(a) are adjacent, m(a) ∈ N(A).

      No two edges of M share an endpoint, so m is one-to-one. Therefore |N(A)| ≥ |m(A)| = |A|. ∎

      Exercise 8. A degree condition

      Let G be bipartite with bipartition (V₁, V₂) and let k ≥ 1. Suppose every vertex of V₁ has degree at least k and every vertex of V₂ has degree at most k. Prove that G has a complete matching from V₁ to V₂.

      Show solution

      Let A ⊆ V₁ and count the edges with an endpoint in A; call this number e.

      Each vertex of A has at least k edges, so e ≥ k|A|. Each of these edges ends in N(A), and each vertex of N(A) has at most k edges, so e ≤ k|N(A)|.

      Hence k|A| ≤ k|N(A)|, and dividing by k > 0 gives |N(A)| ≥ |A|. By Hall's theorem, a complete matching exists. ∎

      Exercise 9. Regular bipartite graphs

      A graph is k-regular if every vertex has degree k. Prove that every k-regular bipartite graph with k ≥ 1 has a perfect matching.

      Show solution

      Every edge has exactly one endpoint in V₁, so |E| = k|V₁|. In the same way |E| = k|V₂|. Hence |V₁| = |V₂|.

      The degree condition of Exercise 8 holds with equality, so there is a complete matching from V₁ to V₂. It has |V₁| = |V₂| edges, so it matches every vertex: it is perfect. ∎

      Exercise 10. Counting matchings

      1. How many perfect matchings does K3,3 have?
      2. How many perfect matchings does Kn,n have?
      3. How many complete matchings from V₁ to V₂ does Km,n have when m ≤ n? What if m > n?
      Show solution

      (a) 6. The first vertex of V₁ has 3 choices, the second 2, the third 1.

      (b) n!. A perfect matching of Kn,n is the same thing as a permutation of n objects.

      (c) n(n − 1)⋯(n − m + 1) = n! / (n − m)!, the number of one-to-one functions from V₁ to V₂. If m > n there are none, since Hall's condition fails for A = V₁.

      Exercise 11. How bad can a maximal matching be?

      Let M be a maximal matching and M* a maximum matching of a graph G. Prove that |M*| ≤ 2|M|. Then give a bipartite graph where equality holds.

      Show solution

      Let S be the set of vertices matched in M, so |S| = 2|M|. Because M is maximal, every edge of G has at least one endpoint in S; otherwise it could be added to M.

      In particular, each edge of M* has an endpoint in S. The edges of M* share no endpoints, so different edges of M* use different vertices of S. Therefore |M*| ≤ |S| = 2|M|. ∎

      Equality: take V₁ = {a1, a2}, V₂ = {b1, b2} and edges a1–b1, a2–b1, a2–b2. Then M = {a2–b1} is maximal with 1 edge, and M* = {a1–b1, a2–b2} has 2 edges.

      Exercise 13. Challenge: a card trick

      A standard deck of 52 cards (13 ranks A, 2, …, K, with 4 cards of each rank) is dealt into 13 piles of 4 cards each. Prove that you can choose one card from each pile so that the 13 chosen cards have 13 different ranks.

      Show solution

      Let V₁ be the 13 piles and V₂ the 13 ranks. Join pile p to rank r if p contains a card of rank r.

      Take any set A of k piles. They contain 4k cards. Each rank has only 4 cards in the whole deck, so these 4k cards need at least 4k / 4 = k different ranks. So |N(A)| ≥ k = |A|.

      By Hall's theorem there is a complete matching from piles to ranks: each pile gets a different rank that it contains. Pick that card from each pile. Since there are 13 piles and 13 ranks, every rank appears exactly once. ∎
