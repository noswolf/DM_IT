The ideas behind the simulation

Definitions

Let G be a connected graph. The vertex connectivity κ(G) is the smallest number of vertices whose removal leaves a disconnected graph or a single vertex. The edge connectivity λ(G) is the smallest number of edges whose removal leaves a disconnected graph. By convention both are 0 for a disconnected graph, and κ(Kn) = n − 1.

A cut vertex is a single vertex whose removal increases the number of components, and a bridge is a single edge with the same property. A connected graph with at least three vertices has κ(G) = 1 exactly when it has a cut vertex, and λ(G) = 1 exactly when it has a bridge. In road terms, one accident at a cut vertex or one fallen tree on a bridge is enough to split the city.

Whitney's inequality

κ(G) ≤ λ(G) ≤ δ(G)

The right-hand inequality is easy to see on the map: take the intersection with the fewest roads, δ(G) of them, and block them all. That intersection is now cut off, so λ(G) can be no larger than δ(G). The left-hand inequality says that closing intersections is at least as powerful as blocking roads: given a smallest set of blocked roads that splits the city, closing a well-chosen end of each one does the same job with no more closures.

Menger's theorem

For two non-adjacent vertices u and v, the largest number of u–v paths that share no vertex except u and v equals the smallest number of vertices whose removal separates u from v. The edge version swaps "vertex" for "edge" in both places. As a consequence, G is k-connected exactly when every pair of vertices is joined by k internally disjoint paths. For a driver, κ is the number of guaranteed independent detours.

Reading the two cities

Barcelona. The Eixample is close to a grid graph. Interior intersections have degree 4 (6 where the Diagonal crosses), but the four corners have degree 2, so κ = λ = δ = 2. There are no cut vertices and no bridges, and independent loops give drivers many alternatives. Between two interior intersections there are 4 internally disjoint routes.

Bangkok. Many sois are dead ends that branch off a main road like small trees. Every dead-end tip has degree 1, so δ = κ = λ = 1. Of intersections, are cut vertices, and of the road segments are bridges. Only independent loops exist, all formed by the main roads and the through-sois Asok, Thong Lo and Ekkamai. Every trip that starts deep in a soi must pass through the soi mouth, which is why the main roads jam even before any roadblock appears: low connectivity concentrates the flow.

"Independent loops" is the cycle rank |E| − |V| + c, where c is the number of components. It counts how many edges you could delete before the graph becomes a forest.

Try this in class

  1. Before blocking anything, compare the average speed and the colour of the main roads in both cities. Ask why Sukhumvit Road and Thong Lo turn amber and red while Barcelona's streets stay clear.
  2. Choose Close an intersection and close one Sukhumvit junction at the mouth of a soi. Watch what happens to the cars inside the soi, then switch on Highlight cut vertices and bridges.
  3. In Barcelona, find the smallest number of closures that disconnects the grid. Students should discover that it is 2 and that the only way to do it is to trap a corner.
  4. Choose Trace disjoint routes and pick two interior intersections in Barcelona, then a dead-end tip in Bangkok and a point on Sukhumvit Road. Compare the counts with Menger's theorem.
  5. Press Worst-case strike in both the intersection mode and the road mode. It blocks a minimum cut, choosing the one that strands the most intersections, and reports the damage.

Discussion questions

Prove that the m × n grid graph (m, n ≥ 2) has κ = λ = 2.

The corners have degree 2, so λ ≤ δ = 2. For κ ≥ 2, remove any vertex x and pick a row and a column that both avoid x. Together they form a connected cross. Any other vertex u lies either in a row that avoids x or in a column that avoids x (it cannot share both with x), so u can travel straight along that line until it meets the cross. Hence no single vertex disconnects the grid, and Whitney gives 2 ≤ κ ≤ λ ≤ 2.

Why is every edge of a tree a bridge, and every non-leaf vertex a cut vertex?

A tree has exactly one path between any two vertices. Deleting an edge uv destroys the only u–v path, so u and v end up in different components. A non-leaf vertex w has two neighbours whose only connecting path goes through w. A dead-end soi network is a tree hanging from one junction, which is why Bangkok's map is full of violet when you turn on the highlight.

Find a graph with κ(G) < λ(G) and describe it as a city.

Two triangles sharing one vertex (the "bowtie") have κ = 1 but λ = 2. As a city, two well-connected neighbourhoods meet at a single roundabout: one accident at the roundabout splits the city, yet you must block two roads to do the same with roadblocks.

How many new road links would make the Bangkok model 2-edge-connected?

Shrink every 2-edge-connected piece to a single point. The bridges then form a tree, and Eswaran and Tarjan (1976) showed that ⌈p/2⌉ new edges are necessary and sufficient, where p is the number of leaves of that tree. In this model p = , so new links are needed, each joining two dead-end tips. This is the graph-theoretic version of the idea of linking neighbouring sois.

Most Eixample streets are actually one-way. Can every street in each model be made one-way while every driver can still reach every destination?

The question asks for a strongly connected orientation, meaning the reachability relation of the resulting digraph is total. Robbins' theorem (1939) says a connected graph has one exactly when it has no bridges. Barcelona's grid qualifies, so a one-way system can keep every intersection reachable. Any bridge in Bangkok's model, such as a dead-end soi, needs two-way traffic or drivers get trapped at one end.

Barcelona's superilla (superblock) programme closes some interior streets to through traffic. Why can a grid afford this?

Block a few interior road segments in the simulation and watch κ and λ. Because interior vertices have degree 4 and the grid has many independent loops, you can delete several interior edges without creating a bridge, so the minimum of 2 set by the corners does not drop. In the soi network almost every segment is already a bridge, so there is no spare capacity to give away.

Model notes

Both maps are stylised, not to scale, and soi numbers are approximate. Every road is treated as two-way. Each driver picks a random destination and, at every intersection, takes the currently fastest route. A road segment slows down as it fills beyond its capacity, following a curve similar to the BPR function used in transport planning, and main roads carry more cars than sois. A driver with no route waits about 3.5 seconds, then picks a new destination. κ(G) and λ(G) are recomputed after every change using maximum flow, so the numbers are exact for the current graph.
